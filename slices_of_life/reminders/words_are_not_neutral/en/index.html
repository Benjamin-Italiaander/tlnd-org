Words are not neutral

In short

Whoever systematically speaks of Jewish settlers instead of Israeli settlers quietly turns a territorial conflict into an opposition between Jews and Palestinians.

Terms such as settler and West Bank also carry their own political and historical weight, and can hide the centuries-old Jewish connection with Judea and Samaria from view.

Journalistic care therefore calls for one simple rule:

Only mention religion or ethnicity when it is relevant — and apply the same standard to Jews as to everyone else.

Words are never entirely innocent. Certainly not in reporting on Israel.

Anyone who reads Dutch newspapers or listens to the public broadcaster increasingly gets the impression that “Jewish” and “Israeli” are used as more or less interchangeable terms.

Especially in reporting on the West Bank, the phrase “Jewish settlers” keeps turning up. Not Israeli settlers, but Jewish settlers.

That may seem like a detail. It is not.

When is religion relevant?

When an Israeli citizen commits a crime in the West Bank, his Israeli identity is what is relevant to the political context of the story. After all, it is about Israel and the Palestinians: about territory, settlements, the Israeli government and the Israeli army.

So why add the word “Jewish”?

When a Dutch person with a Muslim background commits a serious traffic offence, we do not call him a “Muslim driver” without journalistic need. A Catholic farmer who threatens an environmental officer does not become a “Catholic farmer”, and a Protestant fraudster does not become a “Protestant fraudster”.

And rightly so.

Religion and ethnicity should only be mentioned when they are relevant to understanding the news.

Why should a different journalistic standard apply to Jews?

Words carry a history

The effect of that choice of words is also greater than people may realise.

Words such as “settler”, “colonial”, “racist” and “imperialist” have long ceased to be purely descriptive terms in the debate about Israel. They carry a political history.

From the second half of the twentieth century onwards, the Soviet Union in particular had a great influence on the vocabulary used internationally to describe Israel: Zionism was deliberately linked to imperialism, colonialism and racism, Israel was portrayed as a Western colonial outpost, and the Jewish national movement was eventually even described as a form of racism.

Moscow did not invent the word “settler”. But it did help spread internationally a political vocabulary in which Israel and Zionism were placed in these categories from the outset.

The Soviet Union disappeared, but a remarkable part of its political vocabulary remained.

The propagandists understood what such words can do:

Whoever first declares someone a colonist, an imperialist or a racist has already condemned him in public opinion before his arguments have been heard.

The label contains the verdict.

What does “settler” mean?

The word “settler” therefore deserves scrutiny itself.

Today it is used as if it were a neutral description, while it can already contain a historical and political conclusion. It evokes the image of a stranger who moves from a colonial mother country into a territory with which he has no historical bond.

In Judea and Samaria in particular, that image does not do justice to history.

Jews did not only start living there in 1967. Long before the founding of the State of Israel, there were Jewish communities in places such as Hebron, Gush Etzion and Neve Yaakov, among others.

During and around the 1948 war, those communities disappeared from the area that came under Jordanian rule. Gush Etzion was captured and its Jewish villages were destroyed. The Jewish population of the Old City of Jerusalem was expelled.

Jordan gained control of Judea and Samaria and East Jerusalem and annexed those areas in 1950. For nineteen years, Jewish life in the Jordanian-controlled area was gone.

To put it in the terminology of our time: parts of this area were cleansed of their Jewish population.

After 1967, Jews returned to some of those places.

Resettlement and new settlements

One can argue at length about the legal status of the Israeli settlements founded after 1967, and one can oppose Israel’s settlement policy.

But it is historically misleading to present every Jew who lives in Judea and Samaria today, with the loaded word “settler”, as a stranger who has settled on someone else’s land without any historical background.

In some cases it is about resettlement in places where Jewish communities existed before 1948; in other cases it is about new Israeli communities.

That distinction matters.

Whoever uses the same political label for both erases it.

Judea and Samaria, or the West Bank?

The name of the area is also part of this battle over language.

“Judea” and “Samaria” are not modern Israeli inventions, nor are they Netanyahu’s propaganda terms. They are names with a history of thousands of years, known from the Bible and from Greek and Roman sources.

The word “Jew” itself ultimately derives from Judah/Judea. And even people who no longer read a single page of the Bible still know the Good Samaritan.

Yet Judea and Samaria have all but disappeared from much of today’s Western reporting, replaced by “West Bank”, a name that only became common internationally after Jordan had captured the area in the 1948 war.

A journalist may of course use that geographical term. But pretending that “West Bank” is the neutral, self-evident name, and that “Judea” and “Samaria” are merely Israeli political terminology, turns history upside down.

Names shape our image

Names help determine how we understand an area.

Whoever removes Judea and Samaria from the vocabulary risks letting part of the Jewish historical connection with that area disappear from view along with the words.

The mechanism is not fundamentally different from Donald Trump’s attempt to rename the Gulf of Mexico the “Gulf of America”: change the name, and you also try to change the historical and political picture of reality.

Only here it happens in the opposite direction: not by adding a new name to history, but by removing old names from it.

That Judea and Samaria are age-old names does not, in itself, prove who should govern there today under constitutional law.

History is not a land registry.

But the same is true the other way round: a modern geographical name cannot make thousands of years of history disappear either.

The word “Palestinian” changed too

The word “Palestinian” also has a history that is easily lost in today’s terminology.

During the British Mandate, “Palestinian” was not the exclusive national designation for the Arab population that it is today. Palestine was a territorial and political unit in which both Arabs and Jews lived, and Palestinian citizenship existed.

A Palestinian-Arab national consciousness was already developing at the time, but the separate Palestinian national identity as we know it today only took on its strong political form with the founding of the PLO in 1964 and under Arafat.

A large part of the Arab population became refugees in 1948; another part remained within Israel and obtained Israeli citizenship.

Here too, it was not only borders and states that changed, but also the meaning of the words people used for themselves and each other.

Whoever pretends that Palestinian, Israeli, Jew and Arab have always been the same, sharply separated categories reads today’s terminology back into a history that was considerably more complicated.

“Jewish settlers attack Palestinians”

This makes the phrase “Jewish settlers attack Palestinians” even more problematic.

After all, Palestinian is not a religious designation. Palestinians can be Muslim, Christian or atheist.

So the journalist sets a religious-ethnic category against a national one:

Jews versus Palestinians.

Whoever writes “Israeli residents of Judea and Samaria” or, where legally relevant, “Israelis in settlements in the West Bank” describes who is involved and where they live.

Whoever writes “Jewish settlers” does more.

“Jewish” turns the person into an ethnic-religious category, and “settler” immediately sets him against the colonised.

The words then do not only describe reality; they also hand out the roles.

Before a single fact has been discussed, the verdict is ready.

The danger of political language

That is the danger of political language.

A word does not have to be untrue to create a frame. “Settler” is a good example of this. “Jewish settler” goes further: it tells the reader not only who someone is and where he lives, but at the same time suggests who the intruder in the story is.

Whoever first declares his opponent a colonialist, an imperialist and a racist hardly needs to answer his arguments any more.

That does not mean the word “settler” may never be used. It does mean that journalists may be expected to be aware of what words do.

Words do not only describe. They order reality, and can thereby suggest a verdict before the facts have had their say.

“Israeli settlers” is perfectly good Dutch

That “Israeli settlers” (“Israëlische kolonisten”) is perfectly usable Dutch is, incidentally, proven by the media themselves.

The NOS (the Dutch public broadcaster) has used that phrase, and international news agencies generally speak of “Israeli settlers” when nationality and political position are relevant.

Nothing is concealed by this.

The overwhelming majority of Israelis in the settlements in the West Bank are of course Jewish, and religious-Zionist convictions play an important role among certain groups.

When precisely that ideology is the subject of an article, their Jewish identity can be journalistically relevant.

But that is something else than making “Jewish” a standard adjective whenever an Israeli in such a settlement is the subject of negative news.

When words contaminate each other

There is an even more serious consequence.

Whoever constantly links the word “Jewish” to terms that already carry a strongly pejorative weight in public debate — “settler”, “colonial”, “occupier”, “extremist”, “racist” — does not neatly leave that weight with the noun.

Words contaminate each other.

When readers see “Jewish settlers”, “Jewish settlements” and “Jewish violence” again and again, an association can quietly form in which not only the actions are condemned, but the adjective “Jewish” also absorbs part of that negative weight.

No journalist has to be an antisemite for that to happen, and no editorial team needs to have antisemitic intentions.

That is precisely where the danger lies.

Antisemitic imagery needs no antisemitic intent to feed antisemitic associations.

Those who are rightly wary of stigmatising language when it comes to other minorities should recognise this mechanism like no other when it comes to Jews.

Journalistic precision

So this is not about banning words. Nor is it about denying violence by Israelis or settling the legal discussion about the settlements.

It is about something much simpler:

journalistic precision.

Whoever means an Israeli settler can write “Israeli settler”.

Whoever specifically means his religious or ideological conviction can explain why it is relevant that he is Jewish.

But whoever links the word “Jewish” to violence, terror or crime without journalistic need should at least ask himself what image is being created.

One journalistic standard

The question for Dutch newsrooms is therefore ultimately very simple.

If the origin, religion or ethnicity of a perpetrator from other population groups is only mentioned when it is relevant to the news, why should a different rule apply to Jews?

The same newsrooms know how to show restraint when it comes to Moroccan, Syrian or Pakistani origin, or to the Muslim background of a perpetrator.

Then — rightly — the question is asked:

Does that origin or religion matter?

If not, it is left unmentioned.

Why is stigmatisation guarded against for others, while for Jews the group identity is so easily linked to violence?

A journalistic rule that only applies to others is not a journalistic rule. It is a double standard.
